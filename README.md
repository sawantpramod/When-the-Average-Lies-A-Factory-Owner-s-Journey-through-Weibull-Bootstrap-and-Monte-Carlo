# When-the-Average-Lies-A-Factory-Owner-s-Journey-through-Weibull-Bootstrap-and-Monte-Carlo
The Phone Call That Started It All
Ramesh is a composite character, not a specific customer. The dataset and the analysis are real — 50 bulbs from Menon and Agrawal's 2007 filament lamp study (accessed through Allen Downey's survival analysis materials ) but the factory owner is a narrative device.
Ramesh stared at the return slip in his hand. Another customer. Another bulb that died too soon.
His engineer had run the numbers last month: average lifespan, 1,414 hours.
But the returns told a different story. Some bulbs died within weeks. Others lasted for months. The average wasn't wrong — it just wasn't telling him what he needed to know.
I don't want an average," Ramesh muttered, tossing the slip onto his desk. I want to know why they're failing.
That question — why, not how much — is the difference between a number and a model. It's the difference between knowing the temperature and knowing the weather.
1. Classifying the Data First
Before I fit anything, I need to know what kind of data I'm working with. Most analysts skip this step. It's also the one that causes the most problems later.
The data I have is continuous: the lifespan of a bulb, measured in hours. Between 700 hours and 701 hours, there's an infinite universe of possibilities — 700.1, 700.01, 700.001. You can't have "2.5 bulbs," but you can absolutely have a bulb that lasts 2.5 hours.
Continuous data can be modeled with probability distributions — Exponential, Weibull, and others. Each one encodes a different assumption about how failure risk behaves over time. Picking the right one is the whole game.
About the data. The raw file (lamps.csv) contains 33 rows: one origin row at h = 0 with f = 0, and 32 distinct recorded failure times in whole hours, each with a frequency column f and a running survival count K. In total it represents 50 bulbs. Because several h values repeat (e.g. 1,176 hours appears five times), the lifespans are effectively stored as a life table rather than as 50 individually measured values. This has two consequences worth flagging: (i) there are genuine ties in the data, which a continuous distribution assigns zero probability to — a minor effect at this shape and scale, but one to name rather than hide; and (ii) strictly speaking, an interval-censored MLE would be the most faithful treatment if the original binning width were known. Here I treat each repeated value as an exact observation, which is standard practice for this textbook dataset.
Summary
Starting from a single average (1,414 hours), fitting and checking an actual failure-time distribution showed:
The bulbs wear out — they don't fail at a constant random rate. The shape of that wear-out is described by a Weibull distribution with k ≈ 4.27. B10 life is 914 hours — a more useful number for a warranty than the mean or median. The estimated warranty risk at 500 hours is under 1%, with the caveat that this is extrapolated beyond the observed data. The bootstrap 95% CI for the Weibull shape parameter is wide ([3.5, 6.4]), so the exact shape is uncertain even though wear-out is robust. By contrast, propagating parameter uncertainty into the 500-hour warranty probability barely changed it (0.78% to 0.73%).
The average wasn't wrong. It was just incomplete. It said 1,414 hours, and that's accurate. But it couldn't tell me whether the bulbs were failing at random or wearing out — and that distinction is the whole point.
The difference between the engineer's summary and this analysis wasn't intelligence. It was rigor. He computed a mean. I asked whether the model behind it held up.
